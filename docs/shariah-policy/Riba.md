In Islam, **riba** refers to ==the charging or paying of interest on loans, or the unequal, exploitative exchange of specific commodities==. It is strictly forbidden (haram) because it guarantees an unearned, risk-free profit for the lender, disproportionately burdening the borrower and promoting economic injustice.

Riba is primarily divided into two categories:

**Riba al-Nasiyah (Riba of delay):** This is the modern-day equivalent of interest on a loan. It is any predetermined, guaranteed increase over the principal amount that is stipulated in return for waiting or extending a debt's repayment period

**Riba al-Fadl (Riba of excess):** This occurs in spot exchanges of the same commodity (e.g., gold for gold, or dates for dates) where unequal amounts are traded or where the exchange is not immediate. 

The prohibition of riba is considered a core tenet of Islamic commercial law and serves as the foundation for the entire Islamic banking industry. Instead of using interest-bearing loans, Islamic finance facilitates business through Shariah-compliant, asset-backed structures. Common methods to avoid riba include: 

- **Murabaha:** A cost-plus financing arrangement where the bank buys an asset and resells it to the buyer at a known, marked-up price.
- **Mudarabah and Musharakah:** Profit-sharing and partnership agreements where the return to the financier is directly tied to the success or failure of the underlying business venture, thereby linking reward with genuine risk.
- **Ijarah:** Leasing arrangements, such as rent-to-own, where the bank shares ownership and risk until the asset is fully paid off
