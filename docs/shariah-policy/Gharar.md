Gharar is an Arabic term meaning uncertainty, hazard, or excessive risk in a contract. It involves transactions where essential elements—such as the price, quality, quantity, or ability to deliver the goods—are unknown or ambiguous. Islam prohibits it to prevent exploitation, fraud, and disputes between parties

To ensure clarity, transparency, and fairness in all transactions, Islamic law (Sharia) establishes these key principles: 

Why Gharar is Prohibited

The prohibition is rooted in the Quran (e.g., 4:29, forbidding the consumption of wealth wrongfully) and the Hadith. It is forbidden because it can lead to one-sided losses, deception, and situations where consent is based on ignorance rather than mutual agreement

Types of Prohibited Transactions

Contracts are generally considered invalid if they contain substantial gharar. Common examples include:

Selling goods not in possession: Selling unharvested crops, fish in the sea, or unborn livestock

Derivatives and Speculation: Complex financial instruments like options, futures, and short-selling, where the delivery or outcome of the asset is highly uncertain

Gambling: Contracts where a gain for one party directly depends on the loss of another, involving chance rather than a legitimate exchange

Tolerable Risk vs. Excessive Gharar

While all business involves some degree of risk, Islamic jurisprudence distinguishes between minor, unavoidable uncertainty and excessive _gharar_. Minor risks (like standard agricultural yield variances) are generally tolerated, whereas substantial, deceptive risk that undermines the fairness of the contract is strictly forbidden
