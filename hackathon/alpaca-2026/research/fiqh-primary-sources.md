# Fiqh Research: Primary Sources for Options-Structure Allow-List

**Research Date:** 2026-08-19  
**Status:** CRITICAL FINDING — MINORITY POSITION, LIMITED PRIMARY SOURCES  
**Impact on Project Narrative:** See findings below

## Executive Summary

The four option structures in the current allow-list (covered call, cash-secured put, protective put, collar) **do not yet have established Shariah consensus or detailed primary-source justifications in contemporary Islamic finance scholarship**. The project is taking a defensible but explicitly minority position against the mainstream scholarly consensus on derivatives.

### Mainstream Position (Not the project's approach)
- **Mufti Taqi Usmani** (most cited contemporary scholar): Options are **haram** without exception — they involve sale of abstract rights and gambling
- **International Islamic Fiqh Academy (IIFA)** Resolution 238 (9/24, Oct 2018): Options contracts as currently traded are **prohibited**. The resolution states: "Options contracts, as currently traded in the international financial markets, are new contracts that do not fall under any of the Shariah-compliant contracts. The subject matter of options is abstract rights rather than tangible assets." ([source](https://iifa-aifi.org/en/5985.html))

### The Project's Approach
The project frames covered calls, cash-secured puts, protective puts, and collars as **permissible defensive hedging structures** because:
1. The underlying asset is owned or fully cash-backed (reduces gharar)
2. Hedging for genuine risk protection is permitted (per IIFA Resolution 224, Sami Al-Suwailem)
3. Intent is defensive, not speculative

**This position is NOT mainstream Islamic law, and it requires a scholarly advisor to co-author the fiqh justification before publication.**

## Detailed Findings by Strategy

### 1. Covered Call

**Mainstream Position:**
- Mufti Taqi Usmani explicitly rejects covered calls — even if shares are owned, selling a call option involves charging a fee for an abstract promise, which is impermissible
- Sale of a right (the call) separate from the asset violates the Shariah principle that a sale must be for a concrete asset the seller owns

**Closest Islamic Finance Analogue:**
- **Wa'd (unilateral promise)** — A commitment to sell shares at a future date under specified terms. The buyer pays a deposit (arboun) upfront. If exercised, the deposit credits toward the price.
- Not identical to a covered call (which is a right to buy at a fixed price by a date), but structurally allows for the economic outcome

**Permissive Minority Position:**
- Sami Al-Suwailem and other scholars argue that hedging activities (including writing covered calls as portfolio overlay) are permissible if:
  - The agent holds the underlying shares (ownership requirement) ✓
  - The intent is risk mitigation, not speculation ✓
  - No leverage is employed ✓

**Primary Source Gap:**
No AAOIFI standard, IIFA resolution, or detailed scholar opinion *specifically permitting* covered calls exists. The project is extrapolating from general hedging principles.

**Recommended Citation Framework:**
1. Cite IIFA Resolution 224 (8/23, Oct 2018) on hedging as permissible when aligned with Shariah objectives: "Hedging formulas should not involve or pave the way to Riba" and "The real objective of hedging instruments should be the preservation of wealth and not the speculation of prices" ([source](https://iifa-aifi.org/en/6235.html))
2. Cite Sami Al-Suwailem's hedging framework (permits risk management with asset backing)
3. Note that direct permission for "covered calls" does not exist and would require scholar review
4. Document the project as **explicitly taking a minority position justified by hedging principles, not by established options doctrine**

### 2. Cash-Secured Put

**Mainstream Position:**
- Mufti Taqi Usmani: Same as covered call — the put option itself (a fee-charging promise) is impermissible regardless of cash backing
- IIFA Resolution 238: Prohibits all options, including those with collateral

**Closest Islamic Finance Analogue:**
- **Wa'd (unilateral promise)** — A one-way commitment by one party to buy or sell at an agreed rate on a future date, with the other party retaining optionality
- **Wa'dan (bilateral mutual commitment)** — Two unilateral promises creating a two-way binding commitment; considered more just because both parties are bound. Islamic banks use Wa'd/Wa'dan to structure derivative-like FX forwards as conditional promises rather than abstract rights sales. (See: Alisa Ahmad et al., "[The Use of Wa'Dan in Islamic Contract FX Forward](https://ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/ass/article/view/41755)", *Asian Social Science*, 2015)
- For cash-secured puts specifically, this framing transforms the contract from "selling a put option" to "accepting a backed conditional purchase commitment"
- IIFA has noted in Resolution 238 that it **prohibits** hedging structures based on unilateral or bilateral promises, but later resolutions hint that conditional commitments (khayar al-shart) with independent underlying contracts may be permissible

**Permissive Minority Position:**
- Al-Suwailem: Hedging is permitted if the instrument serves genuine risk management and the underlying asset situation is sound
- Some scholars argue that a cash-secured put has **lower gharar** than a naked put because the full value is backed, resembling an Arboun (earnest money) structure more than a speculative derivative

**Primary Source Gap:**
While Wa'd/Wa'dan structures are documented in Islamic finance (used for FX forwards), their application as a framework for "cash-secured put" equivalents is not standardized. The project is reframing a conventional derivative as an Islamic structure.

**Recommended Citation Framework:**
1. Cite IIFA Resolution 238 discussion of Wa'd-based hedging (noting the prohibition: "Options contracts...are new contracts that do not fall under any of the Shariah-compliant contracts"), but also cite Resolution 224 on hedging principles being permissible when aligned with Shariah objectives
2. Cite Sami Al-Suwailem's [Hedging in Islamic Finance](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ris:irtiop:0217) (Islamic Development Bank Institute, Occasional Paper No. 10) on the framework that permits hedging with proper backing
3. Cite Alisa Ahmad et al. on the distinction between Wa'd (unilateral) and Wa'dan (bilateral mutual commitment) in FX forward structures to show the concept is used in Islamic banking
4. Explicitly state: "This project frames a cash-secured put as a Wa'd-backed commitment, which is a minority framing. Mainstream Islamic law (IIFA Resolution 238) does not currently permit such structures."
5. Recommend: A scholar should review and potentially co-author this justification

### 3. Protective Put

**Mainstream Position:**
- Mufti Taqi Usmani: Buying a put to hedge an existing position is still buying an option, which is impermissible
- IIFA Resolution 238: Prohibits options regardless of hedging intent
- Key quote from Mufti Taqi Usmani: *"It makes no difference whether these contracts are entered into for speculation or for hedging"*

**Closest Islamic Finance Analogue:**
- **Takaful (Islamic insurance)** — A mutual risk-pooling mechanism that serves the same protective function as insurance/hedging
- **Guarantee (Daman)** — A third party's guarantee to cover losses, permissible under Islamic law
- **Parallel Istisna or Salam contracts** — Manufacturing contracts with corresponding hedges, used for real commodity risk management

**Permissive Minority Position:**
- IIFA Resolution 224 (8/23, Oct 2018) on Hedging: "Hedging in general is permitted by Shariah as it fulfills the objective of Shariah, which is protection of property" ([source](https://iifa-aifi.org/en/6235.html)). The resolution requires hedging formulas to avoid riba, gharar, and speculation.
- Al-Suwailem: Hedging activities are permissible when aligned with Maqasid al-Shariah (Shariah objectives) and don't involve speculation. Framework emphasizes that risk management with proper backing is not prohibited.
- Contemporary scholars: Defensive use of options to protect an owned asset has **lower gharar** than naked options, making it more defensible

**Primary Source Gap:**
IIFA and mainstream scholarship do not permissibly address "buying put options" as a strategy. The "protective" framing is not established in Islamic law; scholars treat options as inherently problematic regardless of use case.

**Recommended Citation Framework:**
1. Cite IIFA Resolution 224 on hedging activities being permitted when aligned with Shariah objectives
2. Cite Sami Al-Suwailem on hedging frameworks and risk management
3. Cite examples of Islamic insurance (takaful) as the *preferred* alternative for protection, not options
4. State explicitly: "Islamic finance tradition prefers takaful/insurance over options for hedging. This project proposes options as a permissible alternative only when combined with asset ownership and non-speculative intent."
5. Recommend: Scholar review to document this as a minority position

---

### 4. Collar (Put + Call on Owned Shares)

**Mainstream Position:**
- Mufti Taqi Usmani: Both legs of the collar are impermissible, so the structure as a whole is impermissible
- IIFA Resolution 238: Prohibits multi-leg derivative structures

**Closest Islamic Finance Analogue:**
- **Combined Contracts (Muqarina)** — Islamic law permits combining multiple independent permissible contracts into one transaction, with the principle that permissibility of each component determines permissibility of the whole
- However, combining two prohibited instruments (put + call) does not create a permissible structure. This principle restricts the collar to cases where both covered calls and protective puts are independently defensible as permissible hedging.

**Permissive Minority Position:**
- If covered calls and protective puts are each treated as defensible hedging (per the minority positions above), combining them into a collar is simply a compound hedging structure
- Analogous to Islamic insurance (takaful) + Islamic bond (sukuk) portfolio hedging
- No additional gharar is introduced by the combination if each leg independently satisfies hedging principles

**Primary Source Gap:**
No Islamic finance scholarship addresses "collar" strategies. The term itself doesn't exist in Islamic finance literature.

**Recommended Citation Framework:**
1. Cite IIFA on combining contracts (muqarina) as permissible if each component is permissible
2. Note that each leg (covered call, protective put) must be independently justified before the combination is valid
3. State: "A collar is only permissible if its constituent strategies (covered call + protective put) are first established as permissible hedging. This is a synthetic strategy with no independent Islamic law justification."

## Critical Gaps and Limitations

### What Primary Sources Do NOT Support
- **No AAOIFI standard exists for options** — AAOIFI has not issued standards for options contracts, only principles-based guidance that options are problematic
- **No scholar has specifically permitted covered calls, cash-secured puts, or collars** — these strategies are not named or analyzed in Islamic finance scholarship
- **Mufti Taqi Usmani explicitly forbids all of these** — the most-cited contemporary scholar reaches the opposite conclusion
- **The IIFA's 2019 position is prohibitive, not permissive** — their most recent resolution forbids the structures this project permits

### What Exists (But Is Not a Full Justification)
- **Hedging principles are permitted** with requirements: IIFA Resolution 224 (Oct 2018) establishes that "hedging in general is permitted by Shariah" when it fulfills Shariah objectives of protection of property, with specific requirements to avoid riba, gharar, and speculation. ([source](https://iifa-aifi.org/en/6235.html))
- **Wa'd/Wa'dan structures exist** in Islamic finance and are documented in scholarly literature (used for FX forwards). Their reframing as option-equivalents for hedging is novel. Academic treatment: Alisa Ahmad et al. on the distinction between unilateral Wa'd and bilateral Wa'dan. ([source](https://ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/ass/article/view/41755))
- Urbun/Arboun (earnest money / down payment sale) is recognized in AAOIFI Standard SS 53 with permissible conditions: earnest money is credited toward the price if contract is confirmed; seller may forfeit earnest money if buyer fails during stipulated period; forfeited amount must be part of actual price (not separate penalty); subject must be legitimately saleable; and arboun may be taken on lease contracts with similar conditions. (See: [AAOIFI SS 53 Arboun](https://aaoifi.com/ss-53-arboun-earnest-money/?lang=en)). It is primarily used for down payment sales, though the cash-backing model applies to hedging-structure analogies.
- Khayar al-Shart (conditional options in classical Islamic contract law) recognizes five types of khiyar (option) in sales: Khiyar al-Shart (conditional option), Khiyar al-Tayin (option to specify), Khiyar al-Ruyah (option on inspection), Khiyar al-Ayb (option on defect), and one additional type. Permissible conditions are: explicitly stipulated in the contract, time-limited (parties set revocation period), attached to a concrete sale (not abstract rights), and aimed at protecting weaker parties from harm. (See: [Al-Islam.org — Khayar al-Shart](https://al-islam.org/ms/five-schools-islamic-law-muhammad-jawad-mughniyya/option-include-conditions-khayar-al-shari)). Modern application for financial hedging remains limited.
- **Scholars supporting hedging** under strict conditions: Sami Al-Suwailem ([Hedging in Islamic Finance](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ris:irtiop:0217), Islamic Development Bank Institute, Occasional Paper No. 10) and others argue for permissibility of hedging when aligned with Maqasid al-Shariah and when proper asset backing is in place.

### Scholarly Consensus
**There is no consensus supporting this project's position.** The mainstream view (Mufti Taqi Usmani, IIFA) is prohibitive. The permissive view (minority scholars, hedging principles) allows hedging *in general* but not specifically these option structures.

## Conclusion

**The project's fiqh justification for the allow-list is defensible but not mainstream.** It rests on:
1. The general principle that hedging is permitted
2. The specific conditions that asset ownership and cash backing reduce gharar
3. Minority scholar opinion (Al-Suwailem et al.) that hedging with proper backing is permissible
4. Reframing of conventional derivatives as Islamic structures (Wa'd, Urbun, Khayar)

**This is not a weakness if documented transparently.** Both council models flagged fiqh sourcing as the top risk to credibility. The fix is to:
- Acknowledge this is a minority position
- Ground it in real scholarly sources (hedging principles, Wa'd/Urbun frameworks)
- Recommend Shariah Advisory Board review before production use
- Document the mainstream positions and explain the divergence clearly

**For the hackathon submission, this research should inform a "Shariah Methodology" appendix** explaining the project's position on the options-structure gate and its scholarly foundation, rather than hiding the minority-position nature of the stance.
